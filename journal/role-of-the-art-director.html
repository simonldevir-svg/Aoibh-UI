The art director is the part that doesn't get automated

Aoibh Journal2 min read

AI can now produce fifty logo directions before your coffee cools. That is exactly why the art director matters more, not less.

When production is cheap, judgment is the scarce thing. Someone has to decide which of those fifty directions is right for this brand, this audience and this moment, and just as importantly, why the other forty-nine are wrong. That is the art director's job. It hasn't gone anywhere. It has moved to the centre.

In an AI creative studio, the art director works in three places.

At the start, they read the brief the way a client can't. Clients rarely write perfect briefs. The art director hears what sits underneath, asks the question the brief didn't think to answer, and turns it into direction that the AI and the designer can actually use.

In the middle, they curate. AI drafts quickly and drifts toward the average, which is why so much AI-made work looks alike. The art director pulls it away from the middle: sharpening a direction, rejecting the safe option, pairing a machine's speed with a designer's craft.

At the end, they sign off. Before anything reaches a client, a person who is accountable for the work checks it against the brand: the colours, the type, and the harder-to-measure question of whether it feels right. A checklist can catch the first two. Only a person can answer the third.

This is why we don't describe our process as AI replacing designers. The tools handle the heavy lifting of exploration and variation. People handle taste, context and responsibility.

If you're choosing a studio that uses AI, ask who the art director is, what they actually review, and whether they are allowed to say no to the machine. The answer tells you whether you're buying speed, or speed with judgment.
